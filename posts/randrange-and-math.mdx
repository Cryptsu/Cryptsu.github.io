# The basics

## Mersenne Twister
Python's `random` uses `MT19937`, a [Mersenne Twister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_Twister) variant, as its PRNG. As [this ZKSecurity post](https://blog.zksecurity.xyz/posts/kalmar2026/) puts it:

> By default, it produces 32-bit unsigned integers, and various higher-level random functions are built on top of this primitive.

I will now refer to this primitive as `getrandbits32`, similar to the post. 

<br/>
There's `getrandbits(nbits)`, which takes arbitary non-negative `nbits` as input: 
- If `nbits < 32`, `getrandbits32() >> (32-nbits)` will be returned. 
- If `nbits > 32`, concatenate $\left\lfloor{\frac{\texttt{nbits}}{32}}\right\rfloor$ `getrandbits32()`s + one `getrandbits(nbits % 32)` and return the final value.


<br/>
`randrange(N)` takes input `N`, finds `l` such that $2^\texttt{l} < \texttt{N} < 2^\texttt{l+1}$ then calls `getrandbits(l)` until the returned value is `< N`. This process is called rejection sampling.

<br/>
It's hard to recover Mersenne Twister state from `randrange` outputs alone, as ZKSecurity post puts it eloquently:

> Mersenne Twister is linear over $\mathbb{F}_2$ at the bit level, meaning that an attacker can easily recover its full internal state once `19937` independent output bits are known, rejection sampling obscures the exact alignment between observed outputs. In other words, the gaps between outputs become unknown, so the standard approach no longer applies directly and a different method is required.

<br/>
But there's a well-known method that helps you overcome the obstacle:

> A standard trick for dealing with rejection sampling is to exploit structural relations between specific output indices that arise from the PRNG's internal recurrence.
>
> In particular, every triple of 32-bit outputs at indices `[i, i+396, i+623]` satisfies a specific relation. [...]
>
> [...] One tries many triples of observed outputs and checks whether they satisfy the corresponding consistency relation. Whenever a triple passes the test, it strongly suggests that those three outputs are separated by the exact internal offsets required by the Mersenne Twister recurrence. By collecting enough such relations and combining them with external tools such as an SMT solver, one can recover the missing index alignment and eventually reconstruct the full Mersenne Twister state.

<br/>
However, there's one thing that bugs me. It never quite explain how the relations are found, and how can we apply it to smaller `l < 32`. (?)

<br/>
## Notations
Define a sequence of 32-bit vectors $s$ that represents the internal state of `MT19937`.
- $T$: the matrix associated with the `temper()` function.
- $\overrightarrow{s_i}$: the $i$-th element of $s$.
- $\overrightarrow{o_i}$: the $i$-th invocation of `getrandbits32`: $\overrightarrow{o_i} = T \cdot \overrightarrow{s_i}$.
- $\overrightarrow{a}$: the vector representing the constant `0x9908B0DF`.

I'll use Python's array notation *(i.e, $\overrightarrow{v}\texttt{[i:j]}$, $\overrightarrow{v}\texttt{[i]}$)* to denote a slice, indexing...

<br/>
## Encoding

The convention I use to convert non-negative 32-bit integers $\leftrightarrow$ $\mathbb{F}_2$-vectors is:

$$
\begin{matrix}
\texttt{encode(} \overrightarrow{v} \texttt{)} = \sum_\texttt{i} \overrightarrow{v}\texttt{[i]} \cdot 2^\texttt{i} \\
\\
\overrightarrow{v}\texttt{[i]} \texttt{ == } \texttt{(encode(} \overrightarrow{v} \texttt{) >> i) \& 0x1}
\end{matrix}
$$

# The consistency relation

Here's the relation for `getrandbits(l = 32)` *(detail: link(?))*:
$$
W\overrightarrow{o_i} 
+ \overrightarrow{o_{i+396}} 
+ \overrightarrow{o_{i+623}} 
= \overrightarrow{r} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} 

\tag{1}
$$

where $W, \overrightarrow{r}$ are constant. Only $\overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]}$ is unknown, so we need to brute force it. The LHS is a constant while the RHS has 2 possibilities: ($0$, $\overrightarrow{r}$). For uniformly random inputs, this relation is satisfied with probability $\frac{1}{2^{31}}$.

<br/>
If `0 < l < 32`, we only have $\overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[-l:]}$ for every $i$. So the vectors in the relation must be truncated to be verifiable.

$$
\begin{matrix}
& W\texttt{[-l:,:]} \cdot \overrightarrow{o_i} \\
+ & \overrightarrow{o_{i+396}}\texttt{[-l:]} \\
+ & \overrightarrow{o_{i+623}}\texttt{[-l:]} \\
& = \\
& \overrightarrow{r}\texttt{[-l:]} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]}
\end{matrix}
$$

<br/>
However, $\overrightarrow{o_i}$ still has some unknown bits $\overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[0]}, \cdots, \overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[31-l]}$. Let's expand $W\texttt{[-l:,:]} \cdot \overrightarrow{o_i} = \sum_\texttt{j=0}^{31} W\texttt{[-l:,j]} \cdot \overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[j]}$ and move all the terms with unknown $\overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[j]}$ to the RHS.

Finally, here is the relation modified to work for any `0 < l <= 32`:

$$
\begin{matrix}
  & \sum_{\texttt{j}=32-\texttt{l}}^{31} \text{ } W\texttt{[-l:,j]} \cdot \overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[j]} \\
+ & \overrightarrow{o_{i+396}}\texttt{[-l:]} \\
+ & \overrightarrow{o_{i+623}}\texttt{[-l:]} \\
  & = \\
  & \overrightarrow{r}\texttt{[-l:]} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} \\
+ &
\sum_{\texttt{k}=0}^{31-\texttt{l}} \text{ } W\texttt{[-l:,k]} \cdot \overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[k]}
\end{matrix}
$$
<br/>

As `l` decreases by `1`, the RHS:
- gains `1` more unknown term $\overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[k]}$, while 
- the size of the vectors involved decreases by `1`. 

As a consequence, more random inputs can satisfy the relation, causes more false positives to happen, unfortunately.
<br/>

The relation is completely unusable when *the number of terms on the RHS > the size of the vectors coefficients.* For `l = 32`, these two values are 1 and 32, so it works.

Let's compute the lower bound of `l`: 

$$
\begin{matrix}
         & 1+(32-l) & \leq & 32-(32-l) \\
\implies & 1-l      & \leq & l-32 \\
\implies & 2l       & \leq & 33   \\
\implies & l        & >    & 16   \\
\end{matrix}
$$

<br/>
:::warning{title="But..."}
In reality, as `l` decreases, some of the vectors in the set:
- $\overrightarrow{r}\texttt{[-l:]}$
- $W\texttt{[-l:,0]}$
- $W\texttt{[-l:,1]}$
- $\cdots$
- $W\texttt{[-l:,31-l]}$

may collide, or become linearly dependent more often. This event makes the number of RHS vectors decreases.

But I couldn't really put this analysis into use, because in practice, this event doesn't happen until `l = 18`, and by then, the relation is already unusable because of the high false positives rate.
:::
<br/>

# How to find $W$ and $\overrightarrow{r}$

I never really explained how I found the relation. Turns out this process can be automated.

## Step 1: Create the initial symbolic vector-sum expression

We will start by creating this boring symbolic expression that represents $\overrightarrow{o_{396}} + \overrightarrow{o_{623}}$:
$$
E_0 = 
\begin{matrix}
  & \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{396}}\texttt{[0]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{396}}\texttt{[1]} \\
+ & \cdots \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{396}}\texttt{[30]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{396}}\texttt{[31]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{623}}\texttt{[0]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{623}}\texttt{[1]} \\
+ & \cdots \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{623}}\texttt{[30]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{623}}\texttt{[31]} \\
\end{matrix}
$$
<br/>

## Step 2: Apply the temper map

Define a map `temper_map` that converts 
$ \overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[j]} $ 
*to* 
$ \sum_{\texttt{k} = \texttt{0}}^{31} T\texttt{[j,k]} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_i}\texttt{[k]}$ 
for a particular $i$. Apply this map twice, with $i = \{396, 623\}$, to $E_0$, and obtain this new expression:

$$
E_1 = \texttt{temper\_map(temper\_map(}E_0\texttt{, 396), 623)} = 
\begin{matrix}
  & T\texttt{[:,0]}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{396}}\texttt{[0]} \\
+ & T\texttt{[:,1]}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{396}}\texttt{[1]} \\
+ & \cdots \\
+ & T\texttt{[:,30]}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{396}}\texttt{[30]} \\
+ & T\texttt{[:,31]}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{396}}\texttt{[31]} \\
+ & T\texttt{[:,0]}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{623}}\texttt{[0]} \\
+ & T\texttt{[:,1]}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{623}}\texttt{[1]} \\
+ & \cdots \\
+ & T\texttt{[:,30]}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{623}}\texttt{[30]} \\
+ & T\texttt{[:,31]}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{623}}\texttt{[31]} \\
\end{matrix}
$$

Remember that the input and output of `temper_map` are equal, so $E_1 = E_0$.
<br/>

## Step 3: Merge $s_{396}, s_{623}$ symbols to $s_0, s_{-1}$ ones

To do this, we need to recall the state transition function:

$$
\overrightarrow{s_{i+623}} + \overrightarrow{s_{i+396}}

=

\begin{pmatrix}
\Big\vert \\
\Big\vert \\
\overrightarrow{s_{i}}\texttt{[1:31]} \\
\Big\vert \\
\Big\vert \\
\overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} \\
0
\end{pmatrix}

+

\overrightarrow{a} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_i}\texttt{[0]}
$$

<br/>
Let's create a map `merge_396_623` for a particular $i$ to convert:
- `j <= 29`: 
  $ \overrightarrow{s_{i+396}}\texttt{[j]} +
    \overrightarrow{s_{i+623}}\texttt{[j]}
  $ *to*
  $ \overrightarrow{s_i}\texttt{[j+1]} + 
    \overrightarrow{a}\texttt{[j]} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_i}\texttt{[0]}
  $
- `j == 30`:
  $ \overrightarrow{s_{i+396}}\texttt{[30]} +
    \overrightarrow{s_{i+623}}\texttt{[30]}
  $ *to*
  $ \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} + 
    \overrightarrow{a}\texttt{[30]} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_i}\texttt{[0]}
  $
- `j == 31`:
  $ \overrightarrow{s_{i+396}}\texttt{[31]} +
    \overrightarrow{s_{i+623}}\texttt{[31]}
  $ *to*
  $ \overrightarrow{a}\texttt{[31]} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_i}\texttt{[0]}
  $

<br/>
This map requires a hidden asssumption that $\overrightarrow{s_{i+396}}\texttt{[j]}$ and $\overrightarrow{s_{i+623}}\texttt{[j]}$ must have the same vector coefficient. Luckily, it can be applied safely to $E_1$, because these two symbols always share the same vector: $T\texttt{[:,j]}$. Applying `merge_396_623` with $i = 0$, gives us:

$$
E_2 = \texttt{merge\_396\_623(} E_1 \texttt{, 0)} =
\begin{matrix}
  & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{0}}\texttt{[0]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{0}}\texttt{[1]} \\
+ & \cdots \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{0}}\texttt{[30]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{-1}}\texttt{[31]}
\end{matrix}
$$
<br/>

## Step 4: Apply the untemper map

We now define the map `untemper_map` that does the opposite of `temper_map`: converts 
$ \overrightarrow{s_i}\texttt{[j]} $ 
*to* 
$\sum_{\texttt{k} = \texttt{0}}^{31} T^{-1}\texttt{[j,k]} \cdot \overrightarrow{o_i}\texttt{[k]}$ 
for a particular $i$. Apply to $E_2$ with $i = 0$, we now have:

$$
E_3 = \texttt{untemper\_map(} E_2 \texttt{, 0)} =
\begin{matrix}
  & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{0}}\texttt{[0]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{0}}\texttt{[1]} \\
+ & \cdots \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{0}}\texttt{[30]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{o_{0}}\texttt{[31]} \\
+ & \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix}
    & \cdot & \overrightarrow{s_{-1}}\texttt{[31]}
\end{matrix}
$$

Because $E_3 = E_2 = E_1 = E_0 = \overrightarrow{o_{396}} + \overrightarrow{o_{623}}$, the first 31 vectors terms define the columns of $W$, while the last one define $\overrightarrow{r}$.
<br/>


## Code
Here are the scripts I use: *[find-rel.tar.gz](find-rel.tar.gz)*.

```python
from sage.all import *
from lib.maps import (
    temper_map,
    merge_396_623_map,
    untemper_map

r = vector(GF(2), t['S-1_31'])

# print(W)

```

# Extending the `3`-relation

## Finding the `[0, 792, 1246]`-relation

The KalmarCTF blog says that they've found a `[0, 792, 1246]`-relation from the `[0, 396, 623]` one. Now that we have $W$ and $\overrightarrow{r}$, we can do the same:

Let's multiply $W^{-1}$ to [$(1)$](#the-consistency-relation):
$$
\begin{matrix}
        \overrightarrow{o_i} 
+ W^{-1}\overrightarrow{o_{i+396}} 
+ W^{-1}\overrightarrow{o_{i+623}} 
= W^{-1}\overrightarrow{r} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} \\

   \overrightarrow{o_i} 
+ M\overrightarrow{o_{i+396}} 
+ M\overrightarrow{o_{i+623}} 
=  \overrightarrow{q} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} 
& (M = W^{-1} \texttt{,} \overrightarrow{q} = W^{-1}\overrightarrow{r}) \\

   \overrightarrow{o_i}
= M\overrightarrow{o_{i+396}}
+ M\overrightarrow{o_{i+623}}
+  \overrightarrow{q} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} 
& (2)
\end{matrix}
$$

Applying [$(2)$](#finding-the-0-792-1246-relation) to itself gives you:
$$
\begin{matrix}
\overrightarrow{o_i} = & 
  & M^2\overrightarrow{o_{i+792}} \\
&+& M^2\overrightarrow{o_{i+1019}} \\
&+& M^2\overrightarrow{o_{i+1019}} \\
&+& M^2\overrightarrow{o_{i+1246}} \\
&+& M\overrightarrow{q} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i+395}}\texttt{[31]} \\
&+& M\overrightarrow{q} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i+622}}\texttt{[31]} \\
&+&  \overrightarrow{q} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} 
\end{matrix}
$$

Wow, this gets messy real quick! Luckily, $M^2\overrightarrow{o_{i+1019}}$ is repeated twice, so they cancel out. But you can also see that $\overrightarrow{s_{i+395}}\texttt{[31]}$ and $\overrightarrow{s_{i+622}}\texttt{[31]}$ have the same vector coefficients, so what's stopping us from applying `merge_396_623` with $i = -1$? In the end,

$$
\begin{matrix}
\overrightarrow{o_i} = & 
  & M^2\overrightarrow{o_{i+792}} \\
&+& M^2\overrightarrow{o_{i+1246}} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[0]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} 
\end{matrix}
$$

The RHS has `4` different possibilities. For uniformly random 32-bit outputs, this relation satisfies with probability $\frac{1}{2^{30}}$!
<br/>

Implementation-wise, I did this process in backwards: 

I start from two expressions $\overrightarrow{o_{792}} + \overrightarrow{o_{1019}}$, $\overrightarrow{o_{1019}} + \overrightarrow{o_{1246}}$ then work my way back. Only after applying `merge_396_623_map` to each of them, I add them together.

```python
t0 = oi(792) + oi(1019)
t0 = t0(temper_map(792))(temper_map(1019))
t0 = t0(merge_396_623_map(396))

t1 = oi(1019) + oi(1246)
t1 = t1(temper_map(1246))(temper_map(1019))
t1 = t1(merge_396_623_map(623))

t = t0 + t1
t = t(merge_396_623_map(0))
t = t(merge_396_623_map(-1))
t = t(untemper_map(0))
```

## The Sierpiński triangle

It seems like we can keep applying [$(2)$](#finding-the-0-792-1246-relation) to itself to find new relations of $\overrightarrow{o_i}$ with a new set of $\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}$ on the RHS.

After $d$ expansions, [$(2)$](#finding-the-0-792-1246-relation) becomes *(we're only considering $d \leq 32$)*:
$$
\begin{matrix}
\overrightarrow{o_i} = & 
  & M^d \cdot (\sum_{\Delta}\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}) \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[0]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[1]} \\
&+&    \cdots \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[d-1]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[d-2]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} \\
\end{matrix}

\tag{3}
$$
<br/>

We can see that $\Delta$ is always in the form of $396j + 623k$. However, not every term with $\overrightarrow{o_{i + 396j + 623k}}$ appears.

As we expand [$(2)$](#finding-the-0-792-1246-relation), some $\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}$ that are supposed to be involved in the equation, are magically cancelled out. For instance, the term with $\overrightarrow{o_{i+1019}}$ in the previous section.

<br/>
And I wonder if there's a pattern. This is why I decided to visualize a tree where:

- each node represents an offset $\Delta$.
  - if $\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}$ appears in the equation with $\overrightarrow{o_i}$ through repeated application of [$(2)$](#finding-the-0-792-1246-relation) to itself, it is colored **:text[green]{color=green}**.
  - otherwise, it is colored **:text[grey]{color=grey}**.
- the children of a node are $\Delta+396$ and $\Delta+623$
- the root node is $0$.
<br/>


Here's what the tree looks like for the first 6 levels:

![](dependency-graph-simple.svg)

By construction, level $d$ is the set of offsets $\Delta$ such that $\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}$ *(supposedly)* appears in the expansion of [$(2)$](#finding-the-0-792-1246-relation) after $d$ iterations.

For example, level $3$, which contains $\Delta \in \{1188, 1415, 1642, 1869\}$, corresponds to the relation:

$$
\overrightarrow{o_i} 
= M^3(
    \overrightarrow{o_{i+1188}}
  + \overrightarrow{o_{i+1415}}
  + \overrightarrow{o_{i+1642}}
  + \overrightarrow{o_{i+1869}}
) + \cdots
$$

While looking at level $5$, one may imagine that there's a relation such that:
$$
\overrightarrow{o_i} 
= M^5(
    \overrightarrow{o_{i+1980}}
  + \overrightarrow{o_{i+2207}}
  + \overrightarrow{o_{i+2888}}
  + \overrightarrow{o_{i+3115}}
) + \cdots
$$
<br/>

As we zoom out the tree graph to 16 levels, a familiar picture appears:

![](dependency-graph.svg)

It's the [Sierpiński Triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpi%C5%84ski_triangle) *(but a little bit low-res...)*!

::img[]{src="Sierpinski_triangle.svg" width="200px"}

<br/>
At first, I thought I was hallucinating until I checked the coefficients of the terms in the repeated expansion:

![](dependency-graph-coefficients.svg)

Of course! The underlying structure is a binary Pascal triangle! The terms repeated an even number of times are cancelled out in $\mathbb{F}_2$! The reason why the graph looks like the Sierpiński Triangle is because the binary Pascal triangle is used to approximate it!
<br/>


We still need to brute force the locations of the $\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}$ involved before inputting them to the relation. To reduce the brute force effort, it's important to determine which relations depend on the least amount of $\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}$. This motivates me to **look at the tree levels with as few green nodes as possible**.

<br/>
:::note{title="Gould's Sequence"}
According to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gould%27s_sequence):

*"Gould's sequence is an integer sequence [...] that counts how many odd numbers are in each row of Pascal's triangle."*

<br/>
The $n$-th value in the Gould's sequence is:

$$
2^{\#_1(n)}
$$

where $\#_1(n)$ denotes the number of $1$ digits in $n$'s binary representation.
:::

This means that there are $2^{\#_1(d)}$ :text[green nodes]{color=green} in level $d$. It motivates us to look at $d$ that has low Hamming weight: $2^k, 2^m + 2^n, 2^a + 2^b + 2^c, \cdots$.
<br/>

From now on, instead of referring to a particular recurrence relation by the offsets relative to `i`, I'll use **the level $d$ where the corresponding nodes in the tree lives**. $\Delta_d$ refers to the set of green nodes values.

So instead of saying **`[0,1584,2492]`-relation**, I'll say the **level-`4` relation**, because $\Delta_4 = \{ 1584, 2492 \}$.

<br/>

## Find Level-$d$ Relation Using Code

The process applied to find the `[0, 792, 1246]`-relation *(the level-`2` relation)* can be recursively implemented to find any level-`d` relation.

```python
def get_relation(state_index: int, lvl: int) -> tuple[VectorExpr, VectorExpr]:
    """
    Get relation at level lvl such that:
        lhs = rhs
    """
    if lvl <= 1:
        lhs = rhs = (
              oi(state_index + 396)
            + oi(state_index + 623)
        )
    else:
        lhs0, rhs0 = get_relation(state_index + 396, lvl-1)
        lhs1, rhs1 = get_relation(state_index + 623, lvl-1)

        lhs = lhs0 + lhs1
        rhs = rhs0 + rhs1

    lhs = lhs(temper_map(state_index + 396))(temper_map(state_index + 623))
    lhs = lhs(merge_396_623_map(state_index))
    lhs = lhs(merge_396_623_map(state_index - 1))
    lhs = lhs(untemper_map(state_index))

    return lhs, rhs

d = 5   # Example
get_relation(0, d)
```

# False Positives and How To Avoid Them (not completely though...)

As I said in *[The Consistency Relation](#the-consistency-relation)*, as the number of bits `l` in the input of `randrange` decreases, the number of false positives increases dramatically. But not all hope is lost.
<br/>

## Concatenating Relations

The idea is really simple: 

Merge different relations to form one with bigger vector size. Take [$(3)$](#the-sierpiński-triangle), multiply both sides by $W^d = M^{-d}$ to normalize the coefficients of $\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}$, to get:

$$
\begin{matrix}
W^d\overrightarrow{o_i} = & 
  & \sum_{\Delta \in \Delta_d}\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[0]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[1]} \\
&+&    \cdots \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[d-1]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[d-2]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} \\
\end{matrix}
\tag{4}
$$
<br/>

For any two relations at levels $a < b$, notice each relation can be divided into 3 groups:
- $W^d\overrightarrow{o_i}$
- $\sum_{\Delta \in \Delta_d}\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}$
- $\sum_\texttt{j} \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[j]}$

for which we can merge them as such:

This merge thing is implemented as a `concat_map`: a loop that runs through the terms of the two input expressions and concatenates the vector coefficients of matching symbols.

$$
\begin{matrix}

\begin{matrix}
S_1 = & \sum_{\overrightarrow{v}} \begin{pmatrix} a_0 & a_1 & \cdots \end{pmatrix} \overrightarrow{v} \\
S_2 = & \sum_{\overrightarrow{v}} \begin{pmatrix} b_0 & b_1 & b_2 & \cdots \end{pmatrix} \overrightarrow{v} \\
\end{matrix}

&

\implies

&

\begin{matrix}
S_{1,2} = \texttt{concat\_map(}S_1\texttt{,}S_2\texttt{)} = \\
\sum_{\overrightarrow{v}} \begin{pmatrix} a_0 & a_1 & \cdots & b_0 & b_1 & b_2 & \cdots \end{pmatrix} \overrightarrow{v} \\
\end{matrix}

\end{matrix}
$$
<br/>

We can stack however many `concat_map`s there be. So instead of using just either level-`1` relation, or level-`4` relation, or level-`5` relation to build a check function, we can concatenate them to become a singular level `1,4,5`-relation, which is much longer thus less error prone!
<br/>

This is the final form of the concatenation when we don't use more than the first 33 levels of the tree:
$$
\begin{matrix}

\begin{bmatrix}
\\
W^a \\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
W^b \\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
W^c \\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
\cdots \\
\end{bmatrix}
\overrightarrow{o_i} 

= 

\sum_{\Delta \in \Delta_a}

\begin{pmatrix}
\\
\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}
\\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{2em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
0
\\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{2em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
0
\\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{2em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
\cdots
\end{pmatrix}

+ 

\sum_{\Delta \in \Delta_b}

\begin{pmatrix}
\\
0
\\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{2em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
\overrightarrow{o_{i+\Delta}}
\\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{2em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
0
\\
\begin{matrix} \overline{\hspace{2em}} \end{matrix} \\
\\
\cdots
\end{pmatrix}

+ 

\cdots

\begin{matrix}
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[0]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[1]} \\
&+&    \cdots \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[L-1]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[L-2]} \\
&+&    \begin{pmatrix} \overline{\hspace{4em}} \end{pmatrix} 
       \cdot \overrightarrow{s_{i-1}}\texttt{[31]} \\
\\
& &    \text{(}\texttt{L} = \text{max}(a,b,c,\cdots)\text{)}\\
\end{matrix}

\end{matrix}
$$
